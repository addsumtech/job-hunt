# Non-Standard Candidate Playbook

The judges *detect* a weak career arc, a leveling mismatch, a gap, or thin experience — but detection isn't help. This reference is the **constructive positioning** for the candidates who most need this skill: a strong-but-linear candidate barely needs tailoring; these candidates live or die by it. Every move here is **honest only** — positioning and framing of real facts, never hiding (no date-manipulation, no inflation). Read the relevant section before building the tailoring plan.

**Trigger:** apply the matching playbook when the profile shows any of — an employment gap >6 months, a domain/function switch vs. the posting, a seniority mismatch (the candidate is above or below the role's band), an extended absence/re-entry, very thin experience (student / new-grad / <1 role), a **senior-leadership / executive** profile, a **military-to-civilian** transition, or an **internationally-trained / relocating** candidate whose credentials and titles don't map onto the target market.

## 1. Employment gap

**Screening risk:** an unexplained multi-month gap reads as a question mark; recruiters pattern-match it as risk. The skill *forbids* hiding a gap by altering dates (correct) — so the move is to **neutralise it honestly**, not erase it.

**Honest moves:**
- If the gap has a clean reason (study, caregiving, health-now-resolved, relocation, sabbatical, a failed venture), a **one-line, matter-of-fact note** removes the question mark: "2022–2023 — full-time caregiving; returned to engineering 2023." Brief and unapologetic beats conspicuous silence.
- If the candidate did *anything* relevant during the gap (a course, freelance, open-source, volunteering), surface it as a short entry — it converts blank time into continued engagement. Only real activity.
- For an older, short gap on an otherwise strong CV, switching Experience to year-granularity (not month) is legitimate formatting — **only if not used to hide a recent or long gap.** Never to deceive.

**Do NOT:** stretch adjacent job dates to cover the gap (fraud — see `gap-analysis.md`); over-explain in an apologetic tone; invent gap activity.

## 2. Career-changer (domain or function switch)

**Screening risk:** the CV reads as "wrong field." Recruiters screen for pattern-match, so an unbridged switch is a fast reject — the candidate needs the bridge built *for* the reader.

**Honest moves:**
- **Lead with a summary that names the pivot and the bridge** (this is exactly when a summary earns its place — `cv-craft.md §6`): "Data analyst moving into ML engineering; 4 years turning messy data into production decisions, now building the models on top."
- **Reframe transferable experience in the target's vocabulary** (apply the equivalence test in `gap-analysis.md §2`) — surface the genuinely-transferable core (analytical rigor, stakeholder work, the shared technical substrate) and de-emphasise the domain-specific surface.
- **Use the responsibility-evidence pass** (`gap-analysis.md §1`): find where prior work *demonstrated* the new role's responsibilities even under a different title.
- The **cover letter carries the "why the switch" narrative** — honestly and forward-looking.

**Do NOT:** claim domain experience the candidate lacks; bury the pivot and hope the reader doesn't notice (they will, and distrust it); relabel the old role as the new one.

## 3. Re-entry after an extended absence

**Screening risk:** a multi-year absence (parental, caregiving, health, military, study, sabbatical) plus "are the skills current?" doubt.

**Honest moves:**
- State the absence plainly and **frame the return as deliberate**, not desperate: "Returning to software engineering in 2026 after three years of full-time caregiving; refreshed via [real course/project]."
- **Foreground currency:** any recent reskilling, a portfolio project, or a certification goes near the top to answer the "still current?" question before it's asked.
- Lead the CV with the strongest *pre-absence* achievements (real, dated honestly) — the absence doesn't erase a track record; make it visible.

**Do NOT:** disguise the absence with vague dates; apologise for it; imply continuous employment.

## 4. Over-qualification (applying below your level)

**Screening risk:** recruiters reject over-qualified candidates fast — flight-risk, salary-mismatch, "will be bored." The recruiter judge flags this as a seniority-band mismatch.

**Honest moves:**
- **Down-level the *positioning*, not the facts.** Lead with the experience relevant to *this* role's scope; de-emphasise (don't delete) the most senior/strategic bullets that scream "over-qualified" for a hands-on IC role. You may trim, not falsify.
- The **cover letter addresses the elephant** honestly: why you genuinely want this (hands-on focus, domain switch, work-life fit, a deliberate step) — silence here reads as "didn't notice they're overqualified."
- Don't list a higher title's scope if applying for a lower-scope role and it isn't relevant — relevance-trimming is honest; the title and dates stay true.

**Do NOT:** lower your real title or shorten tenure to look junior (falsification); hide senior experience entirely (it'll surface and read as deceptive).

## 5. Thin experience / new-grad / very junior

**Screening risk:** little work history → the CV looks empty against the must-haves. The fix is **making genuine-but-small work compelling**, never inflating it.

**Honest moves:**
- **Lead with Education + Projects** (`cv-craft.md §1` early-career order) — for a student these *are* the evidence.
- **Treat real projects, theses, coursework, hackathons, and open-source as first-class** — describe them with the same action-verb + outcome bullets as jobs (`cv-craft.md §3`), scoped honestly. Use the auto-fetch from the candidate's repos/papers (`gap-analysis.md §1`) to make them specific.
- **Scale genuine small work without inflation:** "built a class project used by 30 classmates" is real and concrete; "architected an enterprise platform" is a lie. Name the true scope.
- Internships, part-time, TA work, and competitions are real experience — include them.

**Do NOT:** invent jobs or inflate a class project into industry work; pad with irrelevant filler; claim seniority signals that aren't there (the Hiring Manager flags this as a credibility problem, not a strength).

## 6. Senior leader / executive

**Screening risk:** the *opposite* of the new-grad problem. An executive CV that's been squeezed into a 1-page, metric-bullet, IC-style template buries the only things that matter at this level — scope, P&L, transformation, and leadership narrative — and reads as under-scoped. Exec hiring also runs more through search firms and referrals than ATS keyword filters, so keyword density matters less and *demonstrated scope* matters more.

**Honest moves:**
- **Lead with an executive summary** (3–5 lines) that states altitude immediately: scope owned (revenue/P&L, headcount, geographies, function), the kind of leader they are, and one or two signature outcomes. This is the headline a board/CEO/search partner reads first.
- **Add a "Selected Achievements" band** of 3–5 career-defining results *above* the role-by-role history (turnarounds, exits/IPOs, market entries, org scale-ups), each with real scale.
- **Quantify in leadership currency:** revenue grown/owned, budget, headcount and span, margin, market share, fundraising, integration scale. These are the metrics that don't feel bolted-on at this level — they're the job.
- **Surface board/advisory roles, P&L ownership, and transformation mandates** explicitly; include them as their own entries where real.
- **Length latitude:** 2 pages is normal, **3 is legitimate** when roles are genuinely distinct and senior (align with `cv-craft.md §5`). Don't amputate real scope to hit an IC-CV page count — but don't pad either.

**Render recipe:** the renderer has first-class `achievements` (renders as "Selected Achievements") and `board` ("Board & Advisory") sections — use them directly, no relabelling. Put the 3–5 signature results in `achievements` and board/advisory roles in `board`, then promote them with `meta.section_order: [summary, achievements, experience, board, education]` and relabel only the summary: `meta.headings: {summary: "Executive Summary"}`. (The `&` in the Board heading is fine — the LaTeX renderer escapes section labels.)

**Do NOT:** inflate span of control or claim sole credit for an org-wide result that was a team's; list every early-career role in full (compress the oldest into a one-line summary); keyword-stuff — at this level it reads as junior.

## 7. Military-to-civilian transition

**Screening risk:** a civilian recruiter can't decode rank, MOS/trade codes, and military jargon, so a genuinely senior, high-responsibility record reads as illegible. The skill must **translate, not inflate.**

**Honest moves:**
- **De-jargon every title and duty into civilian equivalents** the target industry uses: command/leadership scope → "led a 40-person team / managed $5M of equipment"; logistics, operations, intelligence, maintenance → their civilian function names. Keep the real rank/role truthfully, but gloss it ("Sergeant First Class (equivalent to operations team lead)").
- **Translate accomplishments into the target role's vocabulary** via the equivalence test (`gap-analysis.md §2`) and the responsibility pass (`gap-analysis.md §1`) — leadership, training, risk management, operating under pressure, and logistics scale are all directly transferable; name them in industry terms.
- **Surface security clearance** where it's a genuine asset (defense, gov-adjacent, some tech) with level and status — but only where it's relevant; omit it where it means nothing.
- **The cover letter carries the bridge** — why this civilian role, what transfers — forward-looking, not a war story.

**Do NOT:** leave acronyms/MOS codes untranslated; overstate combat/leadership scope; claim civilian-specific tools or certifications not actually held; imply a clearance level higher or more current than the real one.

## 8. International / foreign-credential / relocating

**Screening risk:** foreign degrees, employer names the reader doesn't recognise, an unfamiliar title ladder, and an unstated work-authorization status each create doubt. The candidate is strong but the CV doesn't *map* onto the target market.

**Honest moves:**
- **Localize the format to the target market** (`cv-craft.md §2`) — and apply the **personal-data safety interlock**: if the master CV was built for an EU/Asia market and the target is US/UK, strip photo/DOB/nationality/marital status.
- **Make foreign credentials legible:** add a brief equivalency for a degree where helpful ("Diplom (equivalent to MSc)"), name a recognised credential evaluation if one exists (WES, ENIC/NARIC, NCEES) — honestly, only if actually evaluated — and add a one-line gloss for an employer that's a market leader abroad but unknown locally ("Reliance — India's largest private conglomerate").
- **Address work authorization proactively** where it's material: state status honestly if it's an asset ("EU citizen, full work authorization") or note relocation/visa intent; the recruiter judge will otherwise flag the silence as a logistics blocker. Never overstate eligibility.
- **Standardize dates, currency, and titles** to the target market's conventions; map an unfamiliar title to the nearest local equivalent without inflating it.
- **Language:** render in the target market's language where expected (`cv-craft.md §2`); list real language proficiencies honestly (they're often an asset).

**Do NOT:** invent a credential-equivalency or evaluation that wasn't done; overstate work-authorization or imply citizenship not held; relabel a foreign title as a more senior local one; convert a GPA across scales (`cv-craft.md §1` — never convert grading scales).
